Vocabulary

Key Terms & Concepts

CAT — Computerized Adaptive Testing
An exam delivery method where question difficulty dynamically adjusts based on each response. The CISSP CAT begins at moderate difficulty and narrows toward a competency determination — pass or fail — between 125 and 175 questions.
Scaled Score
A score derived by converting raw performance data to a standardized 0–1000 scale. A scaled score of 700 is required to pass. Because the CAT is adaptive, you cannot simply count correct answers — competency across domains determines the outcome.
CBK — Common Body of Knowledge
The ISC² compendium of cybersecurity topics and best practices that forms the foundation of the CISSP exam. The CBK is organized into eight domains, each representing a functional area of information security practice.
Associate of ISC²
A designation earned by candidates who pass the CISSP exam but do not yet meet the five-year experience requirement. Associates have six years to accumulate the required experience and earn full CISSP status.
Endorsement
The post-exam process in which an active CISSP in good standing attests to the candidate's professional experience. Endorsement must be completed within nine months of passing the exam.
CPE — Continuing Professional Education
Credits required to maintain CISSP certification. CISSPs must earn 120 CPE credits per three-year cycle (minimum 40 per year) through training, teaching, writing, conference attendance, and other professional development activities.
AMF — Annual Maintenance Fee
A $125 annual fee paid to ISC² by each CISSP to maintain active certification status. Failure to pay the AMF results in suspension and eventual revocation of the credential.
CISSP Concentrations
Advanced specializations available to CISSPs after earning the base credential: ISSAP (Architecture), ISSEP (Engineering), and ISSMP (Management). Each requires passing an additional exam and maintaining separate CPE requirements.
NDA — Non-Disclosure Agreement
A binding agreement signed by all CISSP candidates before the exam prohibiting disclosure of specific exam questions or content. Violating the NDA also violates the ISC² Code of Ethics and can result in permanent disqualification.
Linear Exam
The non-adaptive version of the CISSP exam offered in languages other than English. Linear exams consist of up to 250 questions with the same six-hour time limit. All questions must be answered; there is no early termination.
Domain Weighting
The percentage of CISSP exam questions drawn from each of the eight domains. Domain 1 (Security & Risk Management) carries the highest weight at 16%; Domains 2 and 8 each carry 10%. Understanding weights helps prioritize study effort.
ISC² Code of Ethics
A mandatory ethical framework that all ISC² members must uphold. It consists of four canons ordered by priority, starting with protecting society and the public good. Violations can result in credential revocation following a peer review process.
Pearson VUE
The authorized testing center network that delivers the CISSP exam. Candidates register through Pearson VUE, select a test location or remote proctoring option, and must comply with strict identification and personal item policies on exam day.
Performance-Based Question (PBQ)
An interactive question type requiring candidates to demonstrate a skill by performing a task — such as dragging items into the correct order, clicking a hotspot on a diagram, or matching terms to definitions — rather than selecting a text answer.

Ten Topics in Depth

Who Should Pursue the CISSP?

The CISSP is not an entry-level certification. It is designed for experienced information security practitioners who have moved — or are moving — into roles that require not just technical execution, but strategic security leadership. The credential is issued by ISC² (the International Information System Security Certification Consortium) and is recognized globally as the benchmark for senior cybersecurity professionals.

If you are a security analyst who wants to advance into management, an IT director responsible for enterprise risk, or a consultant who advises organizations on their security posture, the CISSP is the logical next step. It signals to employers and clients that you understand security at the governance level — not just at the keyboard.

CISSP vs. Other Certifications

Understanding where CISSP fits in the certification landscape helps candidates determine timing and strategy. CompTIA Security+ is an excellent entry-level credential focused on technical tasks — it validates that you can implement controls. The CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker) validates offensive security knowledge. CISM (Certified Information Security Manager) is a management-level credential focused on governance and program management. The SSCP (Systems Security Certified Practitioner) is ISC²’s own practitioner-level cert, one step below CISSP.

CISSP occupies the senior management-track position: it validates that you can design, oversee, and evaluate an organization’s entire information security program. Exam questions reflect this — they consistently favor governance, risk management, and policy-level answers over technical implementation details.

Industries That Value CISSP Most

  • Government & Defense: DOD 8570/8140 mandates CISSP for many IA technical and management roles
  • Financial Services: Banks and insurance firms require CISSP for senior security roles under SOX and GLBA compliance
  • Healthcare: HIPAA-driven environments prize CISSP for security officers and compliance leads
  • Consulting & Professional Services: Big Four and boutique firms list CISSP as a preferred or required qualification
  • Technology Companies: Cloud providers and SaaS companies require CISSP for security architecture and GRC roles
🎯 Is CISSP Right for You?

Ask yourself these questions before committing to CISSP preparation:

  • Do you have at least 3–5 years of hands-on security experience?
  • Do you work with — or aspire to work with — security at the program or policy level?
  • Do you want a globally recognized credential accepted by employers worldwide?
  • Are you comfortable studying a broad range of topics rather than deep-diving into one technical area?
  • Do you understand that the exam tests management judgment, not just technical recall?
  • Are you prepared to commit 3–6 months of structured study time?

If you answered yes to most of these, CISSP is an excellent fit.

CAT — Computerized Adaptive Testing

The CISSP is delivered in English using Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT). Unlike traditional fixed-form exams that present the same set of questions to all candidates, CAT dynamically adjusts question difficulty in real time based on your responses. When you answer a question correctly, the next question is slightly harder. When you answer incorrectly, the next question is slightly easier. The system is continually refining its statistical model of your competency level.

The exam begins at 125 questions. At that point, if the CAT algorithm can determine with statistical confidence that you are either clearly above or clearly below the passing standard, the exam ends. If your performance is close to the passing line, you receive additional questions — up to a maximum of 175. The exam also ends when you reach 175 questions regardless of confidence, or when the 6-hour time limit expires.

Question Types

  • Multiple Choice (Single Best Answer): The most common format. One stem presents a scenario; four answer choices are provided. Only one is the best answer. The other three are plausible distractors. The key word is “best” — two choices may be correct, but one is more correct in the context of risk management and organizational protection.
  • Multiple Response (Select All That Apply): Requires selecting two or more correct answers from a list. There is no partial credit — all correct choices must be selected and no incorrect ones. Read carefully; these are among the most difficult question types.
  • Drag-and-Drop: Tests the ability to sequence steps, match items to categories, or order processes. Common applications include incident response phases, access control model components, and cryptographic process steps.
  • Hotspot: Presents a diagram, network topology, or system image and asks the candidate to click the correct element. Tests spatial and architectural reasoning — such as identifying where a firewall should be placed in a network diagram.
  • Exhibit-Based: Presents a scenario artifact (a policy document excerpt, a log snippet, a diagram) and asks analytical questions based on that artifact. Tests the ability to apply knowledge to real-world artifacts.

How CAT Determines Pass/Fail

The CAT algorithm does not simply count how many questions you answered correctly. It models your competency level using Item Response Theory (IRT). The system estimates your ability on a continuous scale and determines whether that estimate — with sufficient statistical confidence — is above or below the passing standard. This is why some candidates finish in 125 questions and others receive the full 175. Getting more questions does not mean you are failing; it means the algorithm needs more data to make a confident determination.

🎯 Exam Tip

Do not try to gauge your performance by counting questions or estimating difficulty. Many candidates who pass receive all 175 questions. Focus on each question independently — treat every question as if it is the one that determines your result, because in CAT, it very well might be.

Exam Day at Pearson VUE

  • Identification: Two forms of government-issued ID required; the primary ID must have a photo and signature. Names must match your registration exactly.
  • Personal Items: No phones, watches, wallets, or notes in the testing room. All items go in a provided locker.
  • Scratch Paper: Some centers provide an erasable whiteboard or noteboard; paper is not permitted in most locations.
  • Breaks: You may take unscheduled breaks, but the clock does not stop. Use breaks strategically.
  • Result: An immediate unofficial result (pass/fail) is printed at the end of the exam. Official results are delivered through your ISC² candidate portal within a few days.
⚠ Watch Out

Arrive at the testing center at least 30 minutes early. Late arrivals may be turned away without a refund. Bring exactly the ID listed on your registration — name mismatches are grounds for denial of entry.

Scoring & Passing Standard

The CISSP uses a scaled scoring model with a range of 0 to 1,000. The passing score is 700. This does not mean you must answer 70% of questions correctly — scaled scoring converts your performance on adaptive questions into a standardized score that accounts for question difficulty, domain coverage, and statistical confidence.

Because of the adaptive nature of the CAT, two candidates who both score 700 may have answered different questions at different difficulty levels. One might have answered 125 questions; another answered 175. The scaled score normalizes these differences so all scores reflect an equivalent level of demonstrated competency.

What You Cannot Do

Unlike many certification exams, you cannot simply track your progress by counting correct answers during the CISSP CAT. The adaptive system presents questions at varying difficulty, and harder questions carry more statistical weight toward the competency estimate. Answering five difficult questions correctly contributes more to your score than answering five easy questions correctly. This is by design — the system is trying to find the most efficient path to a confident determination of your ability level.

📋 Key Fact

If the exam ends at exactly 125 questions, the algorithm determined your competency level with high statistical confidence — either clearly above 700 (pass) or clearly below 700 (fail). Ending early is not inherently good or bad; it simply means the system had enough data.

Score Reporting After Failure

Candidates who do not pass receive a score report through the ISC² candidate portal within a few business days. This report provides a domain-by-domain performance breakdown showing areas of strength and weakness. This report is extremely valuable for planning a targeted re-study strategy before the next attempt. Do not ignore it — it is a roadmap for your next attempt.

Immediate Result Notification

At the conclusion of the exam, the Pearson VUE testing center prints an unofficial result notification. This slip will indicate either “Pass” or “Fail.” It will not show a numeric score. The official score report and certification status are posted to your ISC² online account within a few business days. The printed slip from the test center is not an official certificate — it is a preliminary notification only.

🎯 Exam Tip

Do not discuss what your result slip says in public or on social media until you receive your official notification. The immediate result is preliminary and, while rarely changed, is not final until ISC² processes and approves your record.

Experience Requirements

To earn the full CISSP designation, candidates must have a minimum of five years of cumulative, paid, full-time work experience in two or more of the eight CISSP domains. This experience must be verified — it cannot be volunteer work, unpaid internships, or academic coursework (though some education waivers apply).

The five years do not need to be consecutive. Experience accumulated over a career in different roles and organizations counts, as long as it is paid and within the defined scope of a CISSP domain. Candidates should keep detailed records of their employment history, job descriptions, and domain-relevant responsibilities.

What Counts as Experience

  • Full-Time Employment: Counts at full rate — 12 months of full-time work = 1 year of experience credit
  • Part-Time Employment: Counts at a pro-rated rate based on hours worked per week relative to a standard 35-hour work week
  • Internships: Paid internships may qualify; unpaid internships do not
  • Contract/Consulting: Paid contract work within a domain counts as professional experience
  • Military Service: Active duty service in qualifying cybersecurity or IT security roles counts

Education & Certification Waivers

ISC² allows candidates to waive one year of the five-year experience requirement through qualifying education or certifications. This reduces the requirement to a minimum of four years of paid experience.

Waiver TypeReduces Requirement By

ISC²-approved credential (e.g., CompTIA Security+, CCNA Security)1 year

The Associate of ISC² Pathway

Candidates who pass the CISSP exam but do not yet meet the five-year experience requirement are designated as an Associate of ISC². This is a formal ISC² designation — not a consolation prize. Associates have six years from the date they pass the exam to accumulate the required experience, earn endorsement, and complete the transition to full CISSP status.

The Associate pathway is ideal for security professionals earlier in their careers who want to demonstrate their knowledge level, get the credential on their resume, and earn the full CISSP as their experience grows. Many employers recognize and value the Associate designation.

💡 Pro Tip

Start documenting your experience now — even before you pass the exam. Collect job descriptions, performance reviews, and employer contact information for every role where you performed security-domain work. The endorsement process will require you to describe specific duties tied to CISSP domains, and accurate records make this much easier.

How ISC² Verifies Experience

ISC² does not directly contact your employer to verify experience in most cases — that is the endorser’s role. Your endorser (an active CISSP in good standing) reviews your application and attests to the accuracy of your stated experience. ISC² may, however, conduct random audits of endorsement applications and request documentation. Misrepresentation of experience is a Code of Ethics violation and can result in permanent disqualification.

The Endorsement Process

Passing the CISSP exam is a major achievement, but it does not immediately make you a CISSP. You must complete the endorsement process within nine months of your exam pass date. After nine months, the exam pass becomes void and you must retake the exam to restart the process.

Finding an Endorser

Your endorser must be an ISC² member in good standing with an active CISSP credential. They do not need to be your supervisor or employer — they can be a colleague, mentor, professor, or professional contact. If you genuinely do not know any active CISSPs, ISC² will endorse your application directly, though this takes longer and involves additional review.

⚠ Watch Out

You cannot endorse yourself. If your endorser’s certification lapses between the time they agree to endorse you and the time they sign your application, the endorsement is invalid. Confirm your endorser’s active status before submitting.

CISSP Concentrations

After earning the CISSP, members can pursue advanced concentrations that demonstrate deep expertise in a specialty area. Each concentration requires passing an additional examination and maintaining separate CPE requirements.

  • ISSAP (Information Systems Security Architecture Professional): For CISSPs specializing in security architecture design and enterprise security frameworks
  • ISSEP (Information Systems Security Engineering Professional): For CISSPs working in government or defense environments applying systems engineering to security
  • ISSMP (Information Systems Security Management Professional): For CISSPs in leadership roles managing security programs, teams, and governance structures

CISSP Exam Retake Policy

Failing the CISSP exam is disappointing, but it is not uncommon — the exam is genuinely difficult and designed to test senior-level judgment. ISC² allows candidates to retake the exam, but imposes mandatory waiting periods between attempts to ensure candidates have adequate time to study and prepare.

AttemptMandatory Wait PeriodNotes
1st Attempt → 2nd Attempt30 daysUse the domain score report from your failure notice to target weak areas
2nd Attempt → 3rd Attempt90 daysRe-evaluate your study approach; consider a structured course or study group
3rd Attempt → 4th Attempt180 daysReflect seriously on gaps in experience or knowledge before attempting again
Maximum Attempts Per Year3 attempts within any 365-day period

Important Retake Rules

  • The exam fee ($749 USD) must be paid for each attempt — there are no free retakes or fee waivers
  • Candidates must re-register through Pearson VUE for each new attempt
  • The nine-month endorsement window does not restart after a retake — it begins from the date you first pass
  • Waiting periods are strictly enforced; ISC² scheduling systems will not allow early booking
  • Candidates who do not pass after multiple attempts should request and carefully review domain score reports for each failed attempt before re-studying
💡 Pro Tip

Treat each retake like an entirely fresh exam. The CAT adaptive engine will present a completely different set of questions calibrated to your demonstrated ability. Domain score reports from previous attempts are invaluable — they show you exactly which domains pulled your performance below the passing line. Build a targeted study plan around those specific domains rather than re-studying everything equally.

🎯 Exam Tip

If you fail the CISSP, request your domain performance report through the ISC² candidate portal immediately. Do not wait. Review it while the exam experience is fresh, note which domains showed “Below Proficiency,” and build a 30–60 day focused study plan before re-registering.

The ISC² Code of Ethics

All ISC² members — including CISSPs — are bound by the ISC² Code of Ethics. Adherence to the Code is not optional; it is a condition of certification. Violation of any canon can result in a formal ethics complaint, peer review investigation, and revocation of the CISSP credential.

The Code consists of a preamble and four canons. The canons are listed in priority order — in a conflict situation, the earlier canon takes precedence. This priority order is also tested on the exam.

The Four Canons (In Priority Order)

📋 The Four Canons
  • Canon 1: Protect society, the common good, necessary public trust and confidence, and the infrastructure.
  • Canon 2: Act honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly, and legally.
  • Canon 3: Provide diligent and competent service to principals.
  • Canon 4: Advance and protect the profession.

The ordering matters. If an employer (your principal, Canon 3) asks you to take an action that harms the public (Canon 1), the Code requires you to protect the public — even at professional cost. This is the “think like a CISO who serves society, not just the employer” mindset embedded throughout the CISSP exam.

Ethics Complaint Process

Any ISC² member can file an ethics complaint against another member. Complaints are reviewed by the ISC² Ethics Committee, which may convene a peer review panel. Possible outcomes range from a written reprimand to permanent revocation of certification. Revoked CISSPs are publicly listed on the ISC² website.

The Exam NDA & Ethics

Every CISSP candidate signs a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) before beginning the exam. The NDA prohibits sharing, reproducing, or discussing specific exam questions or answers in any format — in person, online, or in study groups. Violation of the NDA is simultaneously a breach of the legal agreement with Pearson VUE and a violation of the ISC² Code of Ethics, specifically Canon 2 (act honestly and legally). Consequences include legal action, permanent exam disqualification, and credential revocation.

🎯 Ethics Exam Tip

When CISSP scenario questions involve an ethical dilemma, always protect the public and society first, then the organization, then the profession. If an employer asks you to cover up a breach that affects customers, the Code of Ethics requires you to report it — protecting the public takes precedence over protecting your employer.

Ethics Questions on the Exam

Ethics questions appear throughout the CISSP exam embedded in scenario contexts. You will rarely see a question that says “Which canon applies here?” — instead, you will be presented with a realistic situation and asked what you should do as a security professional. The correct answer almost always aligns with the canon that appears earliest in the priority list. When two answers both seem ethical, choose the one that protects a broader group of people.

Continuing Professional Education (CPE) Requirements

Earning the CISSP is the beginning of a professional commitment, not the end. To maintain active certification status, CISSPs must earn and report Continuing Professional Education (CPE) credits on an ongoing basis. These requirements ensure that CISSP holders keep their knowledge current in a rapidly evolving field.

Group A vs. Group B CPEs

Group A CPEs are activities directly related to one or more of the eight CISSP domains. These are the primary vehicle for demonstrating continued professional growth in information security. Examples include: attending cybersecurity conferences (RSA, Black Hat, ISC² Security Congress), completing online training courses in security topics, earning new security certifications, publishing articles or white papers on security topics, teaching security courses, and participating in authorized security research.

Group B CPEs cover general professional development that is not directly related to CISSP domains but contributes to overall professional effectiveness. Examples include management and leadership training, public speaking courses, and business communication workshops. Group B is capped at 10 credits per three-year cycle.

How to Earn CPE Credits

  • Attending security conferences and seminars (typically 1 CPE per contact hour)
  • Completing online courses or webinars in cybersecurity topics
  • Teaching or instructing a security course (up to 2x the contact hours)
  • Writing and publishing security articles, blog posts, or white papers
  • Speaking at a security conference or professional event
  • Volunteering for ISC² chapter events or security community organizations
  • Listening to approved security podcasts (with documentation)
  • Earning a new certification in a related security domain
  • Participating in ISC² chapter education events

Consequences of Non-Compliance

Failure to earn the required CPEs or pay the AMF results in a certification lapse. ISC² will notify members before their certification lapses, providing an opportunity to make up the deficit. If the deficit is not resolved, the certification is suspended. A suspended CISSP cannot represent themselves as certified. If suspension continues beyond the grace period, the certification is revoked — and the former CISSP must retake the exam to re-earn it.

💡 Pro Tip

Log your CPEs as you earn them through the ISC² member portal at isc2.org. Do not wait until the end of the cycle. Many CISSPs who face lapse issues did so because they accumulated credits but forgot to log them in time. Set a personal goal of 45 CPEs per year to build a safety buffer above the 40-per-year minimum.

The #1 Mindset Shift: Think Like a CISO, Not a Technician

The single most important preparation insight for the CISSP exam is also the one that trips up the most candidates: the exam does not reward technical answers. It rewards management-level, risk-based, policy-driven answers. Many highly skilled security engineers fail the CISSP because they answer questions the way a technician would answer them — with the most technically correct solution — rather than the way a CISO would answer them.

A CISO thinks about risk to the organization and its stakeholders. A CISO asks: “What approach best protects the organization, its assets, and the public, within legal and ethical constraints, at an appropriate cost?” The CISSP exam consistently favors the answer that reflects this governance perspective over the technically sophisticated alternative.

Risk-Based Thinking Framework

When facing a difficult question, apply this decision framework before selecting an answer:

  • Step 1 — Assess: What is the risk or threat in this scenario? What assets are at stake?
  • Step 2 — Prioritize: Which answer best protects the most critical asset or reduces the highest-priority risk?
  • Step 3 — Govern: Which answer aligns with policy, legal requirements, and organizational authority?
  • Step 4 — Act Last: If an answer involves taking a specific technical action, ask whether analysis and planning should come first

Common Exam Traps

⚠ Watch Out — Common Traps
  • The Technical Answer: When a question asks what you should do, the technical implementation is often the wrong choice. Governance, policy, or risk assessment usually comes first.
  • “Best First Step” Questions: The correct first step is almost always to assess, analyze, or report — not to implement a control. Identify before you fix.
  • Absolute Language: Answer choices containing “always,” “never,” “only,” or “all” are almost always wrong. Security rarely deals in absolutes.
  • Two Correct Answers: When two answers are both defensible, choose the one with the broadest organizational and stakeholder protection scope.
  • The Cost Trap: The cheapest solution is not always wrong, but the CISSP favors the most effective risk-reduction approach over the lowest-cost approach.
  • Vendor/Product Specificity: CISSP is vendor-neutral. Answers that name specific vendors or products are almost always distractors.

Time Management During the Exam

  • Target approximately 2 minutes per question on average across the full exam
  • At 125 questions: check your elapsed time — you should have at least 3.5 hours remaining for potential additional questions
  • Flag questions you are uncertain about and return to them — the CAT system allows review of previously answered questions
  • Never leave a question unanswered — there is no penalty for guessing in the CAT model
  • If you feel mentally fatigued, take a 5-minute break (the clock continues running)

Exam Day Checklist

  • Arrive at the Pearson VUE center at least 30 minutes before your scheduled time
  • Bring two valid, government-issued photo IDs matching your registration name exactly
  • Do not bring phones, watches, notes, food, or personal items into the exam room
  • Eat a solid meal before the exam — 6 hours is a long session
  • Get 7–8 hours of sleep the night before; last-minute cramming is counterproductive
  • Review your key terms and domain weights the morning of — nothing new
  • Arrive with a calm, confident mindset: you have prepared for this

After the Exam

If you passed: Celebrate — you earned it. Then begin the endorsement process immediately. Identify your endorser, gather your employment records, and submit your application well within the nine-month window. Do not wait until month eight.

If you did not pass: Request your domain score report through the ISC² portal as soon as it is available. Review it carefully. Build a targeted study plan addressing your lowest-performing domains. Use the mandatory waiting period productively. Many CISSPs passed on their second or third attempt — a failed first attempt is data, not a verdict.

🎯 Closing Exam Tip

CISSP is a marathon, not a sprint. It tests the depth of your security judgment built over years of practice. The candidates who pass are not necessarily the most technically skilled — they are the ones who most consistently think at the governance and risk management level. Build that mindset throughout this workshop and it will serve you well in the exam room and in your career.
