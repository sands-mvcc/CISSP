Domain 5 — 13% of Exam

Identity and Access Management

Controlling who can do what, when, and under what conditions

⏱ 3 Hours Study 📋 10 Core Topics 🎯 13% Weight (CAT format) 📅 Day 3 Afternoon

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the identity lifecycle (provisioning, role movements, access recertification, and deprovisioning) and mitigate lifecycle security risks.
  • Distinguish identification, authentication, authorization, accountability, and auditing as separate, sequential IAM phases.
  • Select authentication factors and methods (biometrics, PINs, tokens, FIDO2 passkeys) matching security needs.
  • Compare and apply access control models: DAC (Discretionary), MAC (Mandatory), RBAC (Role-Based), and ABAC (Attribute-Based).
  • Design and evaluate single sign-on (SSO) and federated identities using Kerberos, SAML, OAuth 2.0, and OpenID Connect.
  • Enforce logical access rules through least privilege, need-to-know, separation of duties, and dual control structures.
  • Manage high-risk identities: privileged administrator accounts, service accounts, shared identities, and emergency break-glass procedures.
  • Describe the operations and structures of directory services (LDAP, Active Directory) and network authentication backends (RADIUS, TACACS+).
  • Implement and audit structured governance programs using JML workflows, role engineering, and continuous access certifications.

Core Lesson Content

Domain Introduction: Identity is the New Perimeter

Welcome to Domain 5: Identity and Access Management (IAM). As organizations transition to hybrid cloud environments, SaaS platforms, and distributed remote workforces, the traditional physical network boundary has dissolved. Today, identity is the new perimeter. Controlling who can access assets, when, and under what conditions is a critical technical control.

Accounting for 13% of the overall CISSP CAT exam, Domain 5 covers the administrative structures, protocols, and mechanisms used to manage human and logical identities across their lifecycle.

🧠 Think Like a Manager
When evaluating IAM controls on the exam, remember that access decisions must be driven by business requirements, least privilege, and compliance mandates. A manager prioritizes central governance and automation over manual, ad-hoc access configurations.

Understanding the logical sequence of IAM operations—Identification, Authentication, Authorization, and Accountability—and knowing where failures occur is key to mastering this domain.

The Identity Lifecycle & JML Framework

The identity lifecycle manages a user's digital identity from initial creation to permanent removal. Security failures at any stage can result in orphaned accounts, unauthorized access, or regulatory non-compliance.

The Joiner-Mover-Leaver (JML) Framework

The JML framework defines the three primary phases of identity changes within an organization:

1. JOINER: Identity created, background checked, least-privilege role assigned.
▼
2. MOVER: Role changes. Mandatory access review: grant new rights, revoke previous rights.
▼
3. LEAVER: Separation. Accounts disabled immediately; tokens and physical keys collected.
  • Joiner: A new employee or contractor joins the organization. The identity record is created, and baseline privileges are assigned based on their role. Key risk: Over-provisioning access 'just in case' instead of enforcing least privilege.
  • Mover: An existing user changes departments or receives a promotion. Their access rights must be adjusted. Previous role permissions must be explicitly revoked, and only new permissions granted. Key risk: Privilege Creep (the accumulation of legacy access over time).
  • Leaver: An employee separates from the organization. All account access must be disabled immediately to prevent unauthorized access or retaliatory actions. Key risk: Orphaned accounts remaining active after departure.
⚠️ Watch Out
Privilege Creep is a high-value exam topic. When a user transitions between roles, previous permissions must be revoked before or immediately after new permissions are assigned. Access cleanup cannot wait for annual recertification cycles.

Provisioning and Deprovisioning Best Practices

Identity provisioning should leverage Identity Governance and Administration (IGA) platforms to automate account creation based on authoritative HR triggers. Deprovisioning must be immediate upon separation, disabling access across all connected systems, cloud directories, and service accounts. Deactivating accounts rather than deleting them immediately preserves the audit trail for forensic reviews.

Authentication Factors and Metrics

Authentication verifies a user's claimed identity. Robust authentication designs combine multiple factors to defend against credential harvesting, phishing, and replay attacks.

The Three Primary Authentication Factors

  • Type 1: Something You Know: Passwords, passphrases, PINs, or security answers. They are cheap to implement but are vulnerable to brute-force attacks, phishing, and reuse across services. Passphrases (long sequences of random words) are preferred over complex, short passwords.
  • Type 2: Something You Have: Smart cards, hardware tokens (e.g., YubiKey), software authenticator apps (TOTP), or SMS OTP codes. SMS-based OTP is vulnerable to SIM-swapping and SS7 intercept attacks and should be deprecated in favor of authenticator apps or FIDO2 security keys.
  • Type 3: Something You Are: Physiological biometrics (fingerprints, iris scans, retina patterns, facial recognition). Biometrics are non-transferable but cannot be changed if compromised.

Evaluating Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

True MFA requires selecting factors from at least two different categories. Requiring both a password and a PIN is not MFA—both are Something You Know (Type 1). FIDO2/WebAuthn is the current standard for phishing-resistant MFA. It uses public-key cryptography to bind credentials to specific domains, preventing attackers from intercepting and replaying credentials.

Core Access Authorization Principles

Authorization defines what actions an authenticated user is permitted to perform. Enforcing authorization relies on several core principles to minimize risk and prevent fraud.

1. Least Privilege

Users and systems should be granted only the minimum necessary permissions required to perform their specific business functions. Restricting privileges limits the potential damage (the "blast radius") of a compromised account.

2. Need-to-Know

Need-to-know restricts access to specific records or datasets based on immediate, authorized business tasks. For example, two employees with the same security clearance may have different access levels; an HR analyst has no need-to-know accounting databases, and an accountant has no need-to-know employee health records.

3. Separation of Duties (SoD) vs. Dual Control

  • Separation of Duties: Splits a sensitive transaction into multiple sequential steps executed by different people to prevent fraud or error. Example: The employee who requests a purchase order cannot be the same employee who approves the payment.
  • Dual Control: Requires two or more authorized individuals to act together simultaneously to complete a high-risk operation. Example: Launching a missile or opening a safe vault requires two keyholders to turn keys at the same time.

4. Aggregation Risk

Aggregation occurs when a user combines multiple non-sensitive privileges that together create an inappropriately powerful, sensitive permission set. Regular entitlement reviews are critical for identifying and remediating aggregation risks.

🎯 Exam Tip
Authentication verifies who the user is. Authorization determines what they are allowed to do. A scenario describing a user successfully logging in but being blocked from accessing a folder is an authorization event, not an authentication failure.

Access Control Models

Access control models define how authorization decisions are made and enforced across systems. Choosing the right model depends on the organization's security goals and operational environment.

1. Discretionary Access Control (DAC)

In a DAC model, the resource owner has full discretion to grant or revoke access permissions to other users. Example: Standard Windows/Linux file permissions where the file creator sets read, write, or execute permissions for owner, group, and others. This model is highly flexible but is the least secure, as it relies on individual security decisions and is vulnerable to Trojan horse attacks.

2. Mandatory Access Control (MAC)

MAC compares subject clearance labels to object classification labels (such as Secret or Top Secret) based on a central, unchangeable policy. Individual resource owners cannot override MAC policies. MAC is used in high-security, military, and government environments where confidentiality is paramount.

3. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

RBAC assigns permissions to specific organizational roles (such as Finance Analyst or Helpdesk Technician), and users inherit these permissions by being assigned to these roles. This model reduces administrative overhead, simplifies auditing, and scales easily in large enterprise environments.

4. Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)

ABAC is a highly flexible model that evaluates attributes of the subject (e.g., department, role), the resource (e.g., classification), and the environment (e.g., location, time, network threat level) to make real-time, context-aware access decisions. Example: "Permit billing database access only to Accounting roles connecting from a corporate device during business hours." ABAC is the foundation of Zero Trust architectures.

Federation & Single Sign-On (SSO)

SSO allows users to authenticate once to a central identity provider and access multiple connected applications without re-authenticating. Federation extends this capability across different organizations and cloud boundaries.

1. Kerberos: Enterprise Network Authentication

Kerberos is a centralized symmetric-key protocol used in Windows Active Directory environments. It authorizes access using dynamic tickets, ensuring passwords are never transmitted across the network. Clock skew between systems must be under 5 minutes to prevent replay attacks.

  • Authentication Service (AS): Verifies initial user credentials and issues a Ticket-Granting Ticket (TGT).
  • Ticket-Granting Service (TGS): Exchanges the TGT for a specific Service Ticket.
  • Target Service: Accepts the Service Ticket to authorize user access.

2. Federated Identity: SAML vs. OIDC / OAuth 2.0

Federated architectures use trust relationships between an Identity Provider (IdP) and a Service Provider (SP):

  • SAML (Security Assertion Markup Language): An XML-based standard used to securely exchange authentication and authorization assertions between IdPs and SPs. Commonly used in enterprise SSO environments.
  • OAuth 2.0: An open authorization framework that permits third-party applications to access resources on behalf of a user without sharing their credentials. OAuth 2.0 handles authorization, not authentication.
  • OpenID Connect (OIDC): An identity layer built on top of OAuth 2.0 that provides secure user authentication using JSON Web Tokens (JWT).
💡 Pro Tip: OAuth vs. OIDC
Remember the functional distinction: OAuth 2.0 handles authorization (delegating API permissions), while OpenID Connect (OIDC) adds an identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0 to handle authentication (proving user identity).

Directory and Network Authentication Services

Directory services store corporate identity records, while network authentication protocols manage secure access for network endpoints, wireless portals, and administrators.

1. Lightweight Directory Access Protocol (LDAP)

LDAP is an open, hierarchical protocol used to query, search, and manage directories. Users are organized using Distinguished Names (DNs), such as: CN=Jane Doe, OU=Security, DC=enterprise, DC=com. To prevent credentials from being transmitted in cleartext over port 389, organizations must enforce LDAPS (LDAP over TLS) on port 636.

2. Active Directory (AD) Security Risks

Active Directory is a primary target for attackers. Compromising a Domain Controller (DC) grants complete administrative control over all domain-joined computers and users. Common Active Directory attack techniques include:

  • Golden Ticket: Attackers compromise the KRBTGT account password hash to forge dynamic Kerberos Ticket-Granting Tickets (TGTs), establishing persistent, undetectable administrative access.
  • Kerberoasting: Attackers harvest Kerberos service tickets for service accounts associated with Service Principal Names (SPNs) and crack their passwords offline.

3. AAA Protocols: RADIUS vs. TACACS+

RADIUS and TACACS+ provide centralized Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA) for network access:

  • RADIUS: Primarily used for network user access (such as Wi-Fi or VPNs). It encrypts only the password field and combines authentication and authorization into a single exchange. Uses UDP ports 1812 and 1813.
  • TACACS+: Primarily used for network device administration (such as managing routers and switches). It encrypts the entire session payload and separates AAA into distinct exchanges for granular control. Uses TCP port 49.

Privileged Access Management (PAM)

Privileged accounts (such as root, local administrator, or Domain Admin) pose significant security risks because they can bypass security controls and modify system configurations.

1. Types of Privileged Accounts

  • Privileged Admin Accounts: Individual administrator accounts used for system maintenance. They must be kept separate from the user's standard, daily-use account. Administrators should never browse the web or read email with a privileged account.
  • Service Accounts: Used by automated applications to communicate with other platforms. They are often granted excessive privileges and rarely rotated, making them high-value targets.
  • Shared Accounts: Accounts used by multiple personnel. Shared accounts are a critical security risk because they destroy individual accountability and prevent audit attribution.
  • Break-Glass (Emergency) Accounts: Pre-provisioned, highly privileged accounts set aside for critical incidents or system lockouts. Using a break-glass account triggers immediate, high-priority alerts, and all actions are logged in detail.

2. Just-in-Time (JIT) Access and Vaulting

To reduce risk, organizations should implement Privileged Access Management (PAM) solutions. PAM vaults rotate passwords automatically, record privileged sessions, and enforce Just-in-Time (JIT) access. JIT access grants elevated privileges only when requested for an approved task, automatically revoking them when the task is complete or the time window expires. This eliminates standing privileges and reduces the attack window.

⚠️ Watch Out
Shared administrative accounts are unacceptable. If an exam scenario involves multiple administrators using a shared local administrator account, the correct remediation is to eliminate the shared account and implement individual accounts to establish accountability.

Access Governance & Entitlement Audits

Access governance is the operational discipline that ensures access controls remain correct and compliant over time. Without governance, permissions accumulate, leading to excessive privileges and security gaps.

1. Access Recertification Campaigns

Access recertification is a periodic, formal process where managers, data owners, or system owners review and validate that employees' access privileges remain appropriate and justified. The frequency of these campaigns is driven by resource sensitivity:

  • Privileged Access: Minimum quarterly reviews.
  • Standard User Access: Annual reviews.
  • High-Sensitivity Data (e.g., Financials, PII): Quarterly or semi-annual reviews.

2. Entitlement Reviews and Role Engineering

Over time, as employees move through the organization, they accumulate permissions from previous roles. Entitlement reviews are comprehensive audits of all assigned access rights, group memberships, and application permissions. These reviews help security teams identify excessive privileges, orphaned accounts, and Separation of Duties (SoD) violations.

Role Engineering is the systematic process of analyzing business tasks to design, define, and optimize role-based permission sets across the enterprise. It should be completed before deploying an Identity Governance and Administration (IGA) tool.

Domain 5 Scenario Practice

Review these four real-world scenarios to understand how Identity and Access Management principles are applied in CISSP exam situations.

Scenario 1 — Enforcing Least Privilege at Onboarding

The Situation: A new accounts payable analyst is onboarded. Her manager requests "full database permissions" to ensure she can perform her job without disruption.

The Managerial Response: Granting full database permissions violates the principle of least privilege. The IAM team must reject the manager's request. Instead, they must map the analyst's account to a predefined Accounts Payable Role that provides read-only access to vendor tables and write-only access to invoice submission forms, granting only the minimum necessary permissions.

Scenario 2 — Remediating Privilege Creep during Transfers

The Situation: An employee transfers from the HR department to the IT Security team. An access audit six months later reveals they still retain full HR database permissions in addition to their new security tools.

The Managerial Response: This is a classic example of privilege creep caused by a failed mover process. The security team must immediately revoke all HR database permissions, as they are not required for the new security role. To prevent future occurrences, the organization must implement a mandatory access review and explicit revocation step into the JML role-transfer workflow.

Scenario 3 — Evaluating Phishing-Resistant MFA Options

The Situation: A financial firm wants to implement MFA for its remote access gateway. The CISO is evaluating SMS-based OTP, TOTP authenticator apps, and FIDO2 hardware security keys.

The Managerial Response: SMS-based OTP is vulnerable to SIM-swapping and SS7 intercept attacks. While TOTP authenticator apps are more secure, they are still vulnerable to real-time proxy-based phishing attacks. FIDO2 hardware security keys are the strongest choice because they are cryptographically bound to the origin domain, providing robust protection against phishing attacks.

Scenario 4 — Establishing Accountability for Shared Accounts

The Situation: An audit reveals that all network engineers share a single local administrator password to manage core switches, preventing individual auditing and attribution.

The Managerial Response: Shared administrative credentials destroy individual accountability and prevent non-repudiation. The security manager must eliminate the shared account. Network engineers must be assigned individual administrative accounts integrated with a centralized directory service (using TACACS+ or RADIUS), ensuring all administrative actions are uniquely attributed and logged.

Domain 5 Summary: Identity & Access Management

Domain 5 addresses the identity lifecycle, authentication methods, authorization models, federated architectures, directory systems, and access reviews that govern secure access across the enterprise. To succeed on the exam, adopt a governance-first mindset: prioritize central directory services, understand how role-based and attribute-based models make access decisions, and enforce least privilege and need-to-know to minimize risks. Ensure that high-risk administrative, service, and emergency accounts are secured using PAM vaults, session recording, and JIT access. Finally, implement periodic access recertification and entitlement reviews to prevent privilege creep, maintain compliance, and keep access structures secure over time.

🎯 3 Key Exam Reminders

  • The IAM Sequence: Remember the sequential phases of IAM: Identification → Authentication → Authorization → Accountability. A failure at one phase degrades subsequent controls.
  • MFA Requirements: True Multi-Factor Authentication requires selecting factors from at least two different categories: Something You Know (Type 1), Something You Have (Type 2), or Something You Are (Type 3).
  • Revocation at Role Change: Privilege creep occurs when employees transfer roles without previous permissions being revoked. Always mandate explicit access revocation during role changes.
